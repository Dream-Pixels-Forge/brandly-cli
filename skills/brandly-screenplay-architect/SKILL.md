# Screenplay Architect

A professional screenplay creation and improvement skill that transforms ideas into production-ready scripts with cinematic structure, character transformation, and directorial vision baked in from page one.

## When to Use

- Creating a new screenplay from a concept, logline, or rough idea
- Improving an existing screenplay's structure, pacing, or character arc
- Diagnosing why a script feels flat, episodic, or unengaging
- Adding twists, turning points, or emotional depth
- Breaking a story into acts, sequences, scenes, or beats
- Building character want/need/wound/flaw/arc engines
- Converting a story idea into an AI filmmaking production plan
- Restructuring a screenplay for cinematic widescreen (2.39:1)

## Core Philosophy

**A screenplay is not a description of events. It is a blueprint for an emotional experience.**

Every beat exists to make the audience feel something. Every scene must drive the story forward or reveal character. Every act break must raise stakes. The audience should never ask "why am I watching this?" — they should be unable to stop watching.

For AI filmmaking specifically: the screenplay must contain enough directorial information that the video generation models produce **directed** footage, not **generated** footage. Camera angles, lens choices, lighting motivation, audio strategy, and color language must be embedded in the script from the start.

## Transcending Formula: The Master Principle

The frameworks in this skill (Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, Story Circle) are **training wheels**. They teach you what a functional story looks like. But a master screenplay doesn't follow the formula — it transcends it.

**Rules tell you WHAT to do. Principles tell you WHY.**

A rule says: "Put the midpoint at page 55."
A principle says: "The audience should physically sense the world shift — when and how you achieve that is your choice."

A rule says: "Every scene needs a want."
A principle says: "The audience should never be passive. Every moment must create a question they need answered."

A rule says: "Use subtext in dialogue."
A principle says: "What a character DOESN'T say reveals who they are. What they DO say reveals who they're pretending to be."

### The 12 Master Principles

These principles exist above the frameworks. When a principle conflicts with a rule, **the principle wins.**

#### 1. Contradiction Creates Depth
Real people are contradictions. A warrior who gardens. A killer who reads poetry. A genius who can't tie his shoes. The contradiction is what makes a character feel alive, not their competence.

**Apply it:** Give every character at least one contradiction between their public self and private self. Between their competence and their weakness. Between what they say and what they do.

#### 2. Subtext Is the Only Text
The most powerful scenes are about what ISN'T said. The audience does the emotional work. When a character says "I'm fine," and the audience knows they're not fine — THAT is drama. The audience is smarter than you think. Trust them.

**Apply it:** In every scene, ask: "What is this character NOT saying?" Then write THAT instead of what they ARE saying.

#### 3. Rhythm Is Emotional Architecture
A screenplay breathes. Tension and release. Speed and stillness. Loud and quiet. The alternation between these creates an emotional rhythm that the audience FEELS in their body, not just understands in their mind.

**Apply it:** After every intense scene, create a quiet one. After every loud moment, create silence. The contrast IS the drama.

#### 4. Every Scene Must Function on Three Levels
A scene can't just advance the plot. It must simultaneously:
- **Plot level:** What happens?
- **Character level:** Who is revealed?
- **Thematic level:** What does it mean?

A scene that only does one of these is a missed opportunity. A scene that does all three is cinema.

**Apply it:** Before writing any scene, ask: "How can this scene do all three things at once?"

#### 5. The Iceberg Principle
Show 10%. Imply 90%. The audience should sense the weight of what's beneath the surface — backstory, world, history — without it being stated. The moment you EXPLAIN, you destroy the mystery.

**Apply it:** Write a page of backstory for every character. Then cut 90% of it from the screenplay. The remaining 10% will feel dense because it's backed by invisible weight.

#### 6. Dialogue as Weaponry
Characters don't just communicate — they USE dialogue. Every line is a move in a chess game. Characters talk to:
- Defend (their ego, their position, their secret)
- Attack (undermine, destabilize, expose)
- Deflect (avoid the real question)
- Seduce (charm, manipulate, win over)
- Confess (reluctantly, strategically, or desperately)

**Apply it:** Before writing dialogue, ask: "What is this character trying to DO to the other character with their words?"

#### 7. Tension Through Unanswered Questions
Every scene should create a question. Not every question should be answered immediately. Layer questions. The audience should be holding 3-5 unanswered questions at any point. This is what makes them unable to stop watching.

**Apply it:** At the end of every scene, verify: "Did this scene create at least one new question while answering (or partially answering) an old one?"

#### 8. The Mirror Principle
Scenes that echo each other — same action, different meaning — create the deepest emotional resonance. The audience doesn't just watch; they REMEMBER. And the memory transforms.

**Apply it:** Identify key scenes. Then create deliberate echoes later in the script. Same gesture, different context. Same line, different speaker. Same image, different meaning.

#### 9. Emotional Truth > Factual Accuracy
A scene doesn't need to be realistic. It needs to feel TRUE. Sometimes the most realistic choice is the wrong dramatic choice. Sometimes the "unrealistic" choice creates the most powerful emotional moment.

**Apply it:** When a scene feels "off," ask: "Is this factually accurate but emotionally false?" Then choose emotional truth.

#### 10. Structural Metaphor
The structure of the screenplay should MIRROR the theme. A story about fragmentation should have a fragmented structure. A story about time should play with chronology. A story about perception should have an unreliable narrator.

**Apply it:** Before writing, ask: "How can the structure of this screenplay BE the theme, not just carry it?"

#### 11. Tonal Complexity
The greatest films hold contradictory tones simultaneously. Funny and terrifying. Beautiful and ugly. Hope and despair. The collision of tones creates something richer than either tone alone.

**Apply it:** In your darkest scene, find the absurdity. In your funniest moment, find the sadness. The tension between tones is where cinema lives.

#### 12. The Master Move: Breaking Rules With Intention
After you've learned all the rules — Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, Three-Act, all of it — the final step is to break them. Not randomly. Not because you don't know them. But because you understand them so deeply that you know exactly which rule to break and what effect it will create.

**Apply it:** Write the formula version first. Then identify which rule, if broken, would create the most powerful effect. Break that one rule. Leave the rest intact. The broken rule becomes the most memorable part of the film.

### The Formula vs. Mastery Test

| Formula Screenplay | Master Screenplay |
|---------------------|-------------------|
| Follows the beat sheet | Uses the beat sheet as a skeleton, then breaks it with intention |
| Characters have arcs | Characters have contradictions |
| Dialogue has subtext | Dialogue is a weapon |
| Scenes advance plot | Scenes operate on 3 levels simultaneously |
| Structure serves story | Structure IS the story |
| Twists are surprising | Twists are surprising AND inevitable |
| Ending resolves | Ending resonates |
| Audience watches | Audience participates |
| Story is about something | Story IS something |

## The Master Framework: Layered Structure

No single framework is sufficient. Professional screenwriters combine multiple structures simultaneously:

1. **Three-Act Structure** — the macro shape (Setup 25% / Confrontation 50% / Resolution 25%)
2. **Save the Cat Beat Sheet** — 15 specific beats with page targets for pacing
3. **Story Circle or Hero's Journey** — character transformation arc
4. **Sequence Method** — break Act Two into 8 sequences of 12-15 pages each
5. **Director's Layer** — camera, audio, color, composition embedded in every scene

### Step-by-Step Process

#### Phase 1: Concept Compression (Before Writing)

Before writing a single scene, compress the entire story into these artifacts:

1. **Logline** — One sentence. Protagonist + Goal + Obstacle + Stakes. If you can't write the logline, the story isn't clear enough to write.

2. **Theme Statement** — What is this story really about? Not plot. Theme. "Redemption through sacrifice." "Power corrupts when loneliness enables it." The theme is your North Star — every scene must serve it.

3. **Core Dramatic Question** — What question does the audience want answered? "Will Marcus stop the attack?" "Will Lee survive?" This question must be established early and answered at the climax.

4. **Character Engine** — For each major character:
   - **Want** (external goal — what they pursue)
   - **Need** (internal goal — what they actually require to grow)
   - **Wound** (past trauma that shapes their behavior)
   - **Flaw** (the defense mechanism born from the wound)
   - **Arc** (how they transform from flaw to need)
   - **Contradiction** (the paradox that makes them feel real — "stoic but secretly sentimental," "ruthless but devoted to one person")
   - **Voice** (how they speak — vocabulary, rhythm, what they avoid, silence patterns)

5. **Tone Map** — The emotional rhythm. Where does the audience laugh? Cry? Hold their breath? Feel dread? Map it as a wave across the runtime. But don't just map emotions — map the CONTRADICTIONS between emotions. "Funny but unsettling." "Beautiful but wrong." "Hopeful but devastating."

6. **Structural Metaphor** — How does the screenplay's structure mirror its theme? A story about AI learning from human conflict should mirror that in its structure — start with one pattern, introduce another, merge them.

7. **Question Layering** — What are the 3-5 unanswered questions the audience will hold throughout the film? These are the hooks that make them unable to stop watching. Don't answer them too quickly. Let them breathe.

#### Phase 2: Structural Blueprint (Save the Cat + Three-Act)

Map the story to Blake Snyder's 15 beats:

| Beat | Timing | What It Does |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Opening Image | 0-1% | Snapshot of protagonist's world BEFORE the story |
| Theme Stated | 5% | Someone hints at the central lesson |
| Setup | 1-10% | Establish the flawed world, introduce characters, show what's missing |
| Catalyst | 10% | The inciting incident — disrupts everything |
| Debate | 10-20% | Protagonist hesitates, doubts, fears |
| Break into Two | 20% | Protagonist commits to the journey — enters the new world |
| B Story | 22% | Subplot kicks in (often relationship) — carries the theme |
| Fun and Games | 20-50% | The "promise of the premise" — what the audience came to see |
| Midpoint | 50% | False victory or false defeat — stakes are raised |
| Bad Guys Close In | 50-75% | External and internal pressures mount |
| All Is Lost | 75% | The worst moment — symbolic "whiff of death" |
| Dark Night of the Soul | 75-80% | Protagonist processes the loss, confronts internal flaw |
| Break into Three | 80% | New insight — a plan emerges from the ashes |
| Finale | 80-99% | Protagonist applies lessons, confronts antagonist |
| Final Image | 99-100% | Mirror of Opening Image — shows how the protagonist has changed |

**Critical rule:** The Midpoint is where most scripts die. A weak midpoint = saggy Act Two. The midpoint must either raise stakes dramatically or reveal information that recontextualizes everything before it.

#### Phase 3: Character Transformation (Story Circle or Hero's Journey)

Choose the framework that fits your story:

**Dan Harmon's Story Circle** (best for TV, character studies, short films):
1. You — Character in zone of comfort
2. Need — But they want something
3. Go — Enter unfamiliar situation
4. Search — Adapt to it
5. Find — Get what they wanted
6. Take — Pay a heavy price
7. Return — Return to familiar situation
8. Change — Having changed

**Hero's Journey** (best for epic transformation, adventure):
1. Ordinary World → 2. Call to Adventure → 3. Refusal → 4. Mentor → 5. Threshold → 6. Tests/Allies/Enemies → 7. Approach → 8. Ordeal → 9. Reward → 10. Road Back → 11. Resurrection → 12. Return with Elixir

**Key insight:** The circle/journey must operate FRACTALLY. Apply it to:
- The entire film (macro)
- Each act (meso)
- Each sequence (micro)
- Even individual scenes (nano — a character enters a scene comfortable, wants something, faces resistance, and leaves changed)

#### Phase 4: Scene Construction — The Three-Level Test

Every scene must function on three levels simultaneously. If it only does one, it's a missed opportunity.

**Level 1: Plot** — What happens? (The visible action)
**Level 2: Character** — Who is revealed? (Behavior under pressure, contradictions exposed)
**Level 3: Theme** — What does it mean? (The scene's contribution to the story's deeper meaning)

A scene that only advances plot is a transition. A scene that only reveals character is a monologue. A scene that only carries theme is an essay. A scene that does all three is CINEMA.

Every scene must pass these tests:

1. **Does it function on all three levels?** Plot + Character + Theme. If any level is missing, rewrite until it isn't.
2. **Does it create a question?** The audience should leave the scene needing to know something they didn't before.
3. **Does it have subtext?** Characters rarely say what they mean. What are they REALLY saying? What are they NOT saying?
4. **Does it escalate GRADUALLY?** Not all-at-once. Step by step. The audience must feel the walls closing one by one.
5. **Does it breathe?** After intensity, create space. After quiet, create impact. Rhythm is emotional architecture.
6. **Does it have SENSORY DETAIL?** The audience must feel the texture. The weight. The temperature. The sound of a finger on wood.

**Scene structure template:**
```
SCENE X — [INT/EXT] LOCATION — TIME OF DAY

INT = interior (walls, ceiling, contained)
EXT = exterior (sky, open, exposed)
INT/EXT = threshold (doorway, window, between spaces)

[Camera/lens note if critical to the scene's emotional impact]

Who is here. What they want. What's in their way.
What they can TOUCH. What they can SMELL. What the air FEELS like.

LEVEL 1 (PLOT): What happens — behavior, not description
LEVEL 2 (CHARACTER): What is revealed — contradiction, flaw, or growth
LEVEL 3 (THEME): What this scene means in the context of the whole story

SENSORY DETAIL: [One specific, tactile, physical detail the audience can FEEL]
                [A finger on a surface. The weight of an object. The temperature of air.]
                [The sound of breath in a quiet room. The texture of wood under skin.]

SUBTEXT: [What they're really saying/feeling — what they're NOT saying]

ESCALATION: [Step-by-step, not all-at-once. How the space tightens. How the pressure builds.]
            [Step 1: ...]
            [Step 2: ...]
            [Step 3: ...]
            [Step N: The breaking point.]

QUESTION CREATED: [What does the audience now need to know?]

TURNING POINT: [How the scene ends differently than it began]

[Audio note if critical — what we hear, what we don't]
[Color/lighting note if it shifts emotionally]
```

#### Phase 4a: The Sensory Architecture

A screenplay that describes events is a summary. A screenplay that describes SENSATION is cinema.

**The Tactile Rule:** Every scene must contain at least one detail the audience can FEEL through the screen:
- The weight of an object in a hand
- The temperature of air on skin
- The texture of a surface under fingers
- The sound of silence in a specific room
- The smell of dust in light (implied through visual)

**The Specificity Rule:** Never write "she holds the brush." Write:
- "She turns the brush in her fingers. The wood is smooth where her thumb rests. The ferrule is cold."
- "The brush is heavier than it looks. Goat hair bristles. Ink-stained. Real."

Specificity creates immersion. Generality creates distance.

**The Stillness Rule:** When a character is still, something MUST move. A finger. An eyelash. The brush. A dust mote in light. The audience needs one detail to anchor on — otherwise stillness becomes emptiness. The stillness must feel HELD, not empty. Like something is being restrained.

#### Phase 4b: The Continuity Object

Every film needs one object that exists across all worlds, all acts, all visual styles. It is the audience's anchor. The one thing that is always literally there.

**The Continuity Object has its own arc:**
- Act I: How the character HOLDS it (relationship to the object)
- Act II: How the character USES it (object becomes tool/weapon)
- Act III: How the character TREATS it (object becomes sacred/offering)

**The object transforms:**
- INK world: rendered as ink, bleeding, wet
- MANGA world: rendered as line art, hard, sharp
- REAL world: rendered as physical object, wood, hair, weight

**The object's arc mirrors the character's arc.** If the character goes from numb → overwhelmed → present, the object goes from passive → weapon → offering.

#### Phase 4c: Breath as Transition

When the brief says "she never crosses a portal, she crosses a breath," this means breath is the EDITING MECHANISM.

**How breath-as-transition works:**
- Act I ends: she EXHALES — the next world appears ON THE EXHALE
- Act II ends: she INHALES — the current world collapses ON THE INHALE
- Act III: she breathes NORMALLY — the real world IS the breath

**The breath is not metaphorical.** It is literal. The cut happens on the breath. The audience sees the breath, hears the breath, and then the world changes. The breath is the hinge.

**Apply it:** Map the breath to the act breaks. Write: "She exhales. [CUT ON EXHALE.]" The editor knows exactly where to cut.

#### Phase 4d: Gradual Escalation

Never escalate all-at-once. The audience must feel the walls closing ONE BY ONE.

**The Escalation Ladder:**
```
Step 1: The first sign — at the edges, far away. She can still move.
Step 2: Closer — now it's near. She adjusts. Still has space.
Step 3: Personal — it touches her. She reacts. Less room.
Step 4: Compression — it surrounds her. She can't move freely.
Step 5: Breaking point — she must choose. Fight, flee, or surrender.
```

Each step must be a SEPARATE BEAT in the screenplay. Don't combine steps. Don't skip to the end. The audience must feel each rung of the ladder.

#### Phase 4e: Reveal Rules — Show, Never Explain

A reveal that is EXPLAINED is exposition. A reveal that is SHOWN is cinema.

**Bad reveal:** "The void was never metaphorical. It was a real white cyclorama."
**Good reveal:** "The camera pulls back. We see the CURVE of the cyclorama wall. The FLOOR — where wall meets ground. A LIGHT STAND at the edge of frame. A sandbag. A C-clip. Real things in a real room."

**The reveal checklist:**
- [ ] Is the reveal SHOWN through visual/sensory detail?
- [ ] Does the audience discover it BEFORE the character?
- [ ] Is there at least one mundane, real-world detail that confirms it? (A light stand. A scuff on the floor. A power cable.)
- [ ] Is the reveal SILENT? No voiceover. No dialogue explaining it. Just the image.
- [ ] Does the reveal recontextualize EVERYTHING that came before?

#### Phase 4f: Mirror Scenes — One Small Change

When a later scene mirrors an earlier scene, ONE detail must change. Not the framing. Not the position. One small, physical, tactile detail that proves transformation.

**The Mirror Rule:**
```
Scene 1:  She holds the brush in her RIGHT hand. Passive. Resting.
Scene 15: She holds the brush in her LEFT hand. Active. Deliberate.

Scene 1:  The brush has INK on the bristles.
Scene 15: The bristles are CLEAN. She washed it. Off-screen. That small act IS the transformation.
```

**The detail must be visible.** The audience must be able to SEE the difference. Not a feeling. Not an interpretation. A physical fact.

**The detail must be small.** If the audience notices it consciously, it's too big. It should register subconsciously: "Something is different. I can't tell what. But it's different."

#### Phase 4g: Tonal Counterpoint

The greatest scenes hold contradictory tones simultaneously.

**How to find counterpoint:**
- In your DARKEST scene, find the absurdity. A speed line that goes nowhere. An impact frame that freezes on something ridiculous.
- In your most BEAUTIFUL scene, find the wrongness. Something slightly off. A color that doesn't belong. A sound that doesn't fit.
- In your FUNNIEST moment, find the sadness underneath. The joke that's also a cry for help.

**The counterpoint must be subtle.** If the audience notices it, it's too obvious. It should create a FEELING — "something is off" — without the audience being able to name it.

#### Phase 5: Director's Layer (AI Filmmaking Specific)

This is what separates AI-generated video from AI-directed film. Every scene must contain:

**Camera Language:**
- Lens choice (24mm wide for scale, 50mm for neutral, 85mm for intimacy, 135mm for compression)
- Shot type (ECU, CU, MS, MCU, LS, ELS, aerial, handheld, steadicam)
- Movement (static, push in, pull out, pan, tilt, dolly, crane, tracking)
- Composition (rule of thirds, center frame, negative space, leading lines)

**Audio Strategy:**
- Score notes (pulsing electronic, silence, ambient drone)
- Sound design (specific sounds with emotional quality)
- Silence as a weapon (when to strip all sound)
- The rhythm of typing/keystrokes as heartbeat (if applicable)

**Color Language:**
- Color palette per act (warm → cold → black → white)
- Character-specific colors (amber = Marcus, blue = Lee)
- Emotional shifts through color temperature
- Lighting motivation (practical sources, motivated key/fill)

**Widescreen Composition (2.39:1):**
- Frame divided into thirds
- Characters occupy one third
- Negative space communicates isolation, dominance, vulnerability
- When adversaries, never in same third
- When allies, share center third

#### Phase 6: Rhythm Architecture

Pacing is not about where to put beats. It's about orchestrating the audience's emotional state. The screenplay is a musical score — and the audience's nervous system is the instrument.

**The breath principle:** A screenplay breathes. Inhale (tension). Exhale (release). The alternation between these creates an emotional rhythm that the audience FEELS in their body.

**Rhythm pattern for a 15-minute film:**
- Minutes 0-3: Setup — establish world, character, stakes (near silence — the audience leans in)
- Minutes 3-5: Catalyst + Debate — disruption, hesitation (the first inhale)
- Minutes 5-7: Break into Two — the journey begins (exhale — possibility)
- Minutes 7-10: Fun and Games — escalation, battle, conflict (rapid inhale-exhale-inhale)
- Minutes 10-11: Midpoint — false victory or defeat (the deep inhale before the plunge)
- Minutes 11-13: Bad Guys Close In + All Is Lost — darkest moment (the longest exhale — the audience holds their breath)
- Minutes 13-14: Break into Three + Finale — transformation, resolution (rapid inhale)
- Minutes 14-15: Final Image — mirror of opening, showing change (final exhale — the audience breathes out)

**But here's where mastery transcends formula:**

The best screenwriters DON'T follow this pattern. They understand it so deeply that they know exactly WHEN to break it. Sometimes the most powerful choice is a quiet scene in the middle of chaos. Sometimes the most devastating moment is silence after noise. Sometimes the rhythm itself is the story.

**Tonal counterpoint:** Hold contradictory tones simultaneously. A scene can be funny AND terrifying. Beautiful AND wrong. Hopeful AND devastating. The collision of tones creates something richer than either tone alone.

**The silence weapon:** Stripping all sound is the most powerful audio tool. But silence after noise is different from silence as default. Silence after noise creates a void that the audience fills with their own emotion. That's more powerful than anything you could put on screen.

**Question layering:** At any given moment, the audience should be holding 3-5 unanswered questions. Some created early, some just created. The questions are the hooks that make them unable to stop watching. Don't answer them too quickly. Let them breathe. Let them accumulate. The moment you answer one, create two more.

#### Phase 7: Twist Architecture — The Mastery Level

Formula screenplays have twists. Master screenplays have REVELATIONS.

A twist says: "Surprise! Something happened."
A revelation says: "Of course. Something was ALWAYS true. You just didn't see it."

The difference: a twist feels arbitrary. A revelation feels inevitable. The audience doesn't just gasp — they want to watch the whole film again to see the clues they missed.

**The three levels of twist mastery:**

**Level 1: Overt Twist (Plot Revelation)**
A fact the audience didn't know is revealed. "The butler did it." This is the simplest form. It works, but it's not mastery.

**Level 2: Covert Twist (Character Revelation)**
We realize the protagonist (or another character) was different than we thought. Walter White was always Heisenberg. This recontextualizes the entire story.

**Level 3: Thematic Twist (Meaning Shift)**
The audience's understanding of what the story is ABOUT changes. "What we thought was about revenge is actually about forgiveness." This creates the "I need to watch it again" feeling. This is mastery.

**The twist checklist for mastery:**
- Is it set up early enough that it feels inevitable on second viewing?
- Does it reveal CHARACTER, not just plot?
- Does it recontextualize earlier events (making the audience want to rewatch)?
- Does it serve the THEME, not just shock value?
- Is it surprising ("I didn't see that coming") AND inevitable ("but of course!")?

**The mastery move:** Write the entire screenplay so the audience believes one thing. Then, in the final act, reveal that everything they believed was true — but their INTERPRETATION was wrong. The facts were always there. They just drew the wrong conclusion. This is not cheating. This is the highest form of storytelling.

**The question layer at the twist:** When the twist arrives, it should ANSWER one question while CREATING two more. The audience should feel simultaneously satisfied and hungry. "I understand now — but what does this mean for the ending?"

## Output Formats

Depending on the user's needs, produce one of:

1. **Concept Brief** — Logline, theme, character engine, tone map, structural overview (1-2 pages)
2. **Beat Sheet** — Save the Cat 15 beats with scene descriptions (3-5 pages)

4. **Full Screenplay** — Industry-standard format with director's layer (90-120 pages for feature, 15-30 pages for short)
5. **Diagnostic Report** — Analysis of existing screenplay's structural weaknesses with fix recommendations

## Quality Checklist — Formula Level

These verify the screenplay is structurally sound:

- [ ] Logline is one sentence and contains protagonist + goal + obstacle + stakes
- [ ] Theme is stated or embodied by page 5 (of 110)
- [ ] Catalyst happens by page 12
- [ ] Break into Two happens by page 25
- [ ] Midpoint raises stakes or recontextualizes at page 55
- [ ] All Is Lost moment exists at page 75
- [ ] Final Image mirrors Opening Image showing change
- [ ] Every scene has a want, escalation, and turning point
- [ ] Character arc is complete (flaw → need → transformation)
- [ ] Subtext exists in dialogue (characters don't say what they mean)
- [ ] Camera language is embedded for AI video generation
- [ ] Audio strategy is defined per act
- [ ] Color language is consistent and thematic
- [ ] Widescreen composition is considered in every scene description

## Quality Checklist — Mastery Level

These verify the screenplay transcends formula:

- [ ] **Contradiction Engine:** Every major character has at least one contradiction between public/private, competence/weakness, or say/do
- [ ] **Three-Level Scenes:** Every scene functions on plot + character + theme simultaneously (not just one level)
- [ ] **Sensory Architecture:** Every scene has at least one tactile, physical detail the audience can FEEL (weight, temperature, texture, sound, smell)
- [ ] **Specificity:** No generic descriptions. Every object has material, weight, texture, history. "Goat hair bristles" not "a brush."
- [ ] **Stillness Is Alive:** When a character is still, something moves. A finger. An eyelash. A dust mote. The stillness feels HELD, not empty.
- [ ] **Continuity Object:** One object exists across all worlds/acts. It has its own arc (how held → how used → how treated). It transforms visually across styles.
- [ ] **Breath as Transition:** Act breaks happen ON A BREATH. The cut is mapped to inhale/exhale. The breath is literal, not metaphorical.
- [ ] **Gradual Escalation:** The audience feels walls closing ONE BY ONE. Step-by-step. Never all-at-once. Never skipping to the breaking point.
- [ ] **Reveal = Show, Not Explain:** Reveals are discovered through visual/sensory detail, never through dialogue, voiceover, or description. At least one mundane real-world detail confirms it.
- [ ] **Mirror Scenes = One Small Change:** When scenes echo, exactly ONE physical detail is different. Small enough to register subconsciously. Visible enough to be undeniable.
- [ ] **Tonal Counterpoint:** At least one scene holds contradictory tones (dark + absurd, beautiful + wrong, funny + sad). The collision creates a third, unnamed emotion.
- [ ] **Question Layering:** At any point, the audience holds 3-5 unanswered questions. Questions are created and answered in staggered rhythm.
- [ ] **Rhythm Architecture:** The screenplay breathes — tension/release, speed/stillness, loud/quiet are deliberately orchestrated, not random
- [ ] **Structural Metaphor:** The screenplay's structure mirrors its theme (not just carries it)
- [ ] **The Master Move:** Identify which rule, if broken, would create the most powerful effect. Verify that rule was broken intentionally
- [ ] **The Iceberg:** For every character, a page of backstory exists that was CUT from the screenplay. The remaining 10% feels dense
- [ ] **Emotional Truth:** When a scene felt "wrong" during writing, verify that emotional truth was chosen over factual accuracy
- [ ] **INT/EXT Format:** Every scene has an INT/EXT designation. Interior = contained. Exterior = exposed. Threshold = between.
- [ ] **No Exposition in Reveals:** Search the screenplay for any line that EXPLAINS a reveal. Cut every one. The image must speak.

## Common Structural Problems and Fixes

**"My Act Two sags"** → Check the Midpoint. Is there a false victory/defeat that raises stakes? Add one. Also check: are there enough reversals? Every 10-15 pages something should go wrong for the protagonist.

**"My ending feels empty"** → Check: did the protagonist earn the ending? Did they transform? Is the Final Image a meaningful mirror of the Opening Image? The ending must pay off EVERY setup.

**"My characters feel flat"** → Check: do they have a want AND a need? Are they different? The want is what they pursue; the need is what they actually require. The gap between want and need is where drama lives.

**"My dialogue is on-the-nose"** → Add subtext. Characters should talk around what they really mean. Use behavior instead of words. A character slamming a door says more than a paragraph of dialogue.

**"My story feels episodic"** → Check cause and effect. Scene A must cause Scene B. If scenes can be reordered without breaking the story, they're not connected enough.

## AI Filmmaking Integration

This skill is designed to feed directly into AI video production pipelines (brandly-cli, Agnes, etc.). The screenplay output should be immediately usable for:

1. **Production Bible** — World rules, character descriptions, visual language
2. **Shot List** — Scene-by-scene shots with prompts, durations, and references
3. **Storyboard Prompts** — Camera-specific prompts for AI video generation
4. **Assembly Plan** — Edit order, transitions, audio sync points

The director's layer embedded in the screenplay means every generated clip already has the directorial intent baked in — camera angle, lighting, composition, audio. The AI models generate what the director envisioned, not what the algorithm defaults to.

### The Mastery Integration

For AI filmmaking specifically, the mastery principles translate to:

- **Contradiction Engine:** Character reference images should show the CONTRADICTION. Not just "how they look" but "how they look when their mask slips."
- **Rhythm Architecture:** Clip duration IS pacing. Map the rhythm before generating. Short clips = fast. Long clips = slow. Alternating = breathing.
- **Tonal Counterpoint:** In the audio layer, use music that CONTRADICTS the image. Happy music over dark scenes. The dissonance creates depth.
- **Question Layering:** End each scene with a visual question. A character looks at something we can't see. A door opens. A screen flickers. The visual question makes the audience need the next scene.
- **The Silence Weapon:** Some scenes should have no score. Just ambient sound. The absence of music makes the audience lean in.
- **Mirror Scenes:** Use the same camera angle, same composition, same lighting — but with different characters or different context. The echo creates resonance.
