# Character Engine Reference

## The Character Formula

A complete character has six elements that work together as an engine:

```
WANT (external goal) ←→ NEED (internal requirement)
         ↕                        ↕
    FLAW (defense mechanism) ←→ WOUND (past trauma)
         ↕
      ARC (transformation from flaw to need)
```

### The Six Elements

**1. Want (External Goal)**
- What the character actively pursues
- Observable, measurable, concrete
- "Stop the attack." "Win the competition." "Find the treasure."
- The audience can see this happening

**2. Need (Internal Requirement)**
- What the character actually requires to grow
- Often opposite of the want
- Hidden from the character themselves
- "Learn to trust others." "Accept imperfection." "Forgive himself."
- The audience feels this before the character recognizes it

**3. Wound (Past Trauma)**
- The event that created the flaw
- Happened before the story begins
- Shapes how the character sees the world
- "Betrayed by a partner." "Failed to save someone." "Abandoned as a child."
- Never fully explained in dialogue — revealed through behavior

**4. Flaw (Defense Mechanism)**
- The behavior pattern born from the wound
- How the character protects themselves from being hurt again
- "Trust no one." "Control everything." "Never show weakness."
- The flaw is what must change for the character to grow

**5. Arc (Transformation)**
- The journey from flaw to need
- The character starts at the flaw and ends at the need
- The arc must be earned through accumulated experience
- The climax is where the character finally chooses need over want

**6. Ghost (Backstory)**
- The specific event that created the wound
- Usually shown, never told
- Revealed at the emotional low point (Dark Night of the Soul)
- Reframes everything the audience thought they knew

### Want vs. Need: The Drama Gap

The gap between want and need is where ALL drama lives:

| Character | Want | Need | Gap |
|-----------|------|------|-----|
| Walter White | Money for family | Power and ego | He says family, but he means empire |
| Luke Skywalker | Adventure off Tatooine | Father figure / belonging | He wants to leave, but needs to find where he belongs |
| Marcus Tyler | Win the cyber war | Respect an equal | He fights to win, but needs to recognize Lee as worthy |

**The climax forces the choice:** Want or Need? The protagonist must sacrifice one to get the other.

## Character Arc Types

### 1. Positive Arc (Growth)
Character overcomes flaw, embraces need.
- Start: Flawed, defensive, wounded
- End: Transformed, open, whole
- Example: Luke Skywalker (fear → courage through faith)

### 2. Negative Arc (Corruption)
Character embraces flaw, rejects need.
- Start: Potentially good
- End: Worse than they started
- Example: Walter White (chemistry teacher → drug lord)
- Walter says: "I did it for the family." Truth: He did it for himself.

### 3. Flat Arc (Unchanging)
Character already knows the truth. The world changes around them.
- Start: Already believes the theme
- End: Still believes it — but now the world reflects it back
- Example: James Bond (already competent, the mission changes him temporarily)

### 4. Disillusionment Arc
Character loses their innocence/flaw simultaneously.
- Start: Naive, hopeful
- End: Realistic, hardened
- The theme is about the cost of truth

## Character Introduction: First Impressions

The audience forms an opinion of a character within their first 30 seconds on screen. Use this:

### Snyder's "Save the Cat" Moment
The protagonist does something early that makes the audience like them. Not necessarily heroic — just likeable.

- A guy helps a stranger (heroic)
- A woman makes a joke at her own expense (self-deprecating)
- A kid defends someone weaker (protective)

### Character Introduction Through Behavior

**Don't:** "John is a retired detective who drinks too much."
**Do:** John sits alone at a bar. He stares at his phone. A photo of a woman. He puts the phone face-down. Orders another drink.

Behavior = Character. Everything else is just information.

### The Character Introduction Checklist

- [ ] What do they WANT in this scene? (specific, observable)
- [ ] How do they BEHAVE under pressure? (reveals flaw)
- [ ] What is their BODY LANGUAGE? (wound made visible)
- [ ] What do they AVOID? (defense mechanism)
- [ ] What is their VOICE? (pace, volume, word choice = personality)

## Dialogue as Character

### Subtext Principle

Characters rarely say what they mean. They talk AROUND it.

**On-the-nose dialogue (BAD):**
"I'm angry at you because you betrayed me."

**Subtext dialogue (GOOD):**
"You always did have trouble with loyalty."

The second version reveals:
- Character is angry (but won't say it directly)
- Character values loyalty (by attacking its absence)
- Character is wounded (but shows it through accusation, not confession)
- Character is intelligent (uses precision, not emotion)

### Character Voice

Each character should have a distinct way of speaking:

| Element | Question | Example |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Vocabulary | What words do they use? | Technical vs. casual vs. literary |
| Sentence length | Short and punchy or long and flowing? | Military mind = short sentences |
| What they DON'T say | What do they avoid? | A character who never mentions their past |
| Verbal tics | Do they repeat words? Use filler? | "Basically..." "Look..." "The thing is..." |
| Silence | When do they say nothing? | Silence under pressure = control |

### Dialogue Economy

Every line of dialogue must do at least TWO of these:
1. Reveal character
2. Advance the plot
3. Establish tone
4. Create tension
5. Set up a payoff

If a line does none of these, cut it.

## Ensemble Dynamics

### The Character Web

In a story with multiple characters, each relationship should create friction:

```
Marcus ←→ Lee (rivalry → respect → alliance)
Marcus ←→ The Third Intelligence (threat)
Lee ←→ The Third Intelligence (threat)
```

Each pair should have:
- A shared goal (or opposing goals)
- A power dynamic (who has more? who wants more?)
- An unspoken history (what happened before the story?)
- A turning point (when does the relationship shift?)

### The B Story

The B Story (usually a relationship) carries the theme:

- A Story = Plot (what happens)
- B Story = Theme (what it means)

The B Story character says the theme out loud (usually to the protagonist who doesn't understand it yet).

Example: In PIXELS WAR, the B Story is Marcus and Lee's relationship. They start as rivals. The theme is "the enemy of my enemy is my friend." The B Story resolves when they realize they must work together.

## Character Pressure Checklist

For each major character, verify:

- [ ] Clear want (external goal)
- [ ] Clear need (internal requirement)
- [ ] Defined wound (past trauma)
- [ ] Defined flaw (defense mechanism)
- [ ] Complete arc (flaw → need)
- [ ] Distinct voice (dialogue style)
- [ ] Introduced through behavior (not description)
- [ ] Has subtext in dialogue (doesn't say what they mean)
- [ ] Makes choices under pressure (reveals character)
- [ ] Is changed by the story (arc completes)
