# Director's Toolkit for AI Filmmaking

The screenplay is the director's blueprint. For AI filmmaking, this means embedding directorial information directly into the script so that generated video matches the director's vision, not the algorithm's defaults.

## Camera Language

### Lens Choice (Emotional Meaning)

| Focal Length | Field of View | Emotional Effect | Use When |
|--------------|---------------|------------------|----------|
| 14-24mm | Ultra-wide | Scale, awe, isolation | Establishing shots, showing vastness |
| 24-35mm | Wide | Context, environment | Showing character in their world |
| 35-50mm | Normal | Neutral, objective | Dialogue, everyday moments |
| 50-85mm | Medium telephoto | Intimate, personal | Close-ups, emotional moments |
| 85-135mm | Telephoto | Compressed, claustrophobic | Surveillance, tension, isolation |
| 200mm+ | Super telephoto | Extreme compression | Sniper POV, distance, observation |

### Shot Types (What the Camera Shows)

| Shot | Frame | What We See | Use For |
|------|-------|-------------|---------|
| ECU (Extreme Close-Up) | Eyes, lips, fingers | Detail, emotion, tension | Reveal character, show reaction |
| CU (Close-Up) | Face, head and shoulders | Emotion, thought | Dialogue, reaction, internal state |
| MCU (Medium Close-Up) | Chest up | Expression + some body language | Conversation, subtle emotion |
| MS (Medium Shot) | Waist up | Body language + environment | Action, movement, interaction |
| MLS (Medium Long Shot) | Knees up | Full body + environment | Walking, movement, context |
| LS (Long Shot) | Full body | Character in environment | Establishing, scale, isolation |
| ELS (Extreme Long Shot) | Tiny figure in landscape | Environment dominates | Scale, world-building, isolation |

### Camera Movement (Emotional Meaning)

| Movement | Emotional Effect | Use When |
|----------|------------------|----------|
| Static | Objectivity, observation | Surveillance, control, detachment |
| Slow push in | Building tension, intimacy | Character realization, dread |
| Fast push in | Shock, revelation | Surprise, danger, urgency |
| Slow pull out | Isolation, loneliness, loss | Character alone, aftermath |
| Pan (horizontal) | Following action, revealing | Scanning, searching, connecting |
| Tilt (vertical) | Power, scale | Revealing size, looking up/down |
| Dolly/tracking | Journey, pursuit | Following character, movement |
| Handheld | Instability, chaos, intimacy | Combat, panic, documentary feel |
| Steadicam | Grace, controlled movement | Long takes, confidence, flow |
| Crane | Scale, power, perspective | Aerial reveals, epic moments |
| Aerial/drone | God's eye view, geography | Establishing scale, battlefield |

### Composition (Frame Language)

| Composition | Meaning | When to Use |
|-------------|---------|-------------|
| Rule of thirds | Balance, natural feel | Default for most scenes |
| Center frame | Power, confrontation, importance | Key character moments, face-offs |
| Negative space left | Anticipation, looking forward | Character looking ahead |
| Negative space right | Memory, looking back | Character reflecting |
| Negative space above | Isolation, vulnerability | Character overwhelmed |
| Leading lines | Direction, focus | Guiding eye to subject |
| Frame within frame | Entrapment, observation | Character trapped, watched |
| Dutch angle | Disorientation, wrongness | Something is off, psychological |
| Symmetry | Order, control, beauty | Kubrick-style, institutional |

### The 2.39:1 Widescreen Rule

The frame is divided into thirds. Characters occupy one third. Negative space communicates emotion.

```
|  LEFT THIRD  |  CENTER THIRD  |  RIGHT THIRD  |
|              |                |               |
|  Marcus      |                |      Lee      |
|  (amber)     |   NEGATIVE     |     (blue)    |
|              |    SPACE       |               |
```

**When adversaries:** Never share the same third. They are visually opposed.
**When allies:** Share the center third. Visual unity = narrative unity.
**When dominant:** Character fills the center third. Power.
**When vulnerable:** Character pushed to edge. Isolation.

## Audio Strategy

### Score Architecture

The score is not background music. It is the emotional heartbeat of the film.

**Act-by-Act Score Plan:**

| Act | Score Style | Emotional Function |
|-----|-------------|-------------------|
| Act I | Near-silence. Room tone. Keyboards. Monitors humming. | Establish normal world. Tension through absence. |
| Act II | Low-frequency pulses sync with battle escalation. Bass drops on impacts. | Rising stakes. Physical sensation of conflict. |
| Act III | Frequency rises. Layered electronic textures. Tension through dissonance. | Precision, intimacy, stakes. |
| Act IV | Unnatural frequency. Organic distortion. Wrongness. | The third intelligence is alien. Sound = wrongness. |
| Act V | All frequencies converge. New harmonic from merged colors. | Resolution. Unity. New beginning. |

### Sonic Palette Reference

- **Ben Frost** — Controlled aggression, digital texture
- **Cliff Martinez** — Electronic ambient, emotional undertow
- **Olafur Arnalds** — Cold precision, synthetic beauty

### Sound Design Signatures

Every element in the cyberworld has a signature sound:

| Element | Sound Quality | Emotional Effect |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| Missiles | Digital whistle, pitch rising | Threat, speed, precision |
| Tank engines | Data grinding through code | Power, weight, unstoppable force |
| Sniper rounds | Code crack, digital snap | Precision, lethality, distance |
| Electronic warfare | Cascading interference | Chaos, disruption, blindness |
| The Third Intelligence | Silence → single devastating THUM | Wrongness, alien, overwhelming |

### Silence as a Weapon

Stripping all sound is the most powerful audio tool. Use it for:
- The moment before impact
- Character realization
- The third intelligence's arrival
- Emotional devastation
- The final moment before resolution

## Color Language

### Character Color Assignments

| Character/Element | Primary Color | Secondary | Meaning |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------|---------|
| Marcus (FORGERONE) | Amber/Gold | Black | Warmth, control, methodical |
| Lee (SABER12) | Blue/White | Charcoal | Cold precision, calculation |
| The Third Intelligence | Black | — | Absence, void, alien |
| Unknown/Threat | Red | — | Danger, warning, alarm |
| Alliance | Amber + Blue merging | White | Unity, new frequency, resolution |

### Color Temperature by Act

| Act | Temperature | Emotional Effect |
|-----|-------------|------------------|
| Act I | Warm amber (Marcus) vs. Cold blue (Lee) | Opposition, isolation |
| Act II | Full spectrum — amber, blue, explosions | Escalation, scale |
| Act III | Desaturated, muted tones | Precision, intimacy, danger |
| Act IV | Black absorbs all color | The third intelligence consumes |
| Act V | Amber + Blue = new color (white/gold) | Alliance, resolution, new beginning |

### Lighting Motivation

Every light source must have a motivation — a reason within the scene why the light exists.

**Good motivation:**
- Monitors cast amber glow on Marcus's face (practical source)
- Rain-streaked windows create blue fill (environment)
- Keyboard backlight illuminates hands (practical)

**Bad motivation:**
- Dramatic rim light with no source (it looks staged)
- Perfectly lit face in a dark room (unmotivated)

### Practical Lighting Sources

For cyberpunk specifically:
- Monitor glow (amber/blue depending on character)
- Neon signs through windows
- LED strips on equipment
- Holographic displays
- Circuitry patterns on suits/avatars
- Emergency lighting (red)
- Reflections on rain-wet surfaces

## Continuity System

### Character Continuity

Track for each character:
- Clothing state (damage, wet, torn)
- Physical state (injured, exhausted, focused)
- Emotional state (which stage of their arc?)
- Prop state (holding what, using what?)

### Environmental Continuity

- Weather state (rain, clear, storm)
- Time of day (lighting consistency)
- Damage state (what's been destroyed?)
- Technology state (what's working, what's broken?)

### Match Cuts and Transitions

| Transition | Use When | Emotional Effect |
|------------|----------|------------------|
| Match cut (action) | Character does same action in different location | Continuity, connection |
| Match cut (visual) | Similar shapes/colors across scenes | Thematic link |
| Smash cut | Hard cut from quiet to loud (or vice versa) | Shock, reversal |
| Cross dissolve | Passage of time, connection | Smooth transition, reflection |
| Hard cut | Scene change, time jump | Directness, efficiency |
| Smash to black | Finality, death, ending | Impact, silence |
| Fade from black | Beginning, awakening, hope | Fresh start, new chapter |

## AI Video Generation Integration

### Prompt Structure for AI Video

Every shot prompt should follow this structure:

```
[SHOT TYPE] -- [CAMERA MOVEMENT] -- [LENS] -- [CHARACTER DESCRIPTION] -- 
[ACTION] -- [ENVIRONMENT] -- [LIGHTING] -- [COLOR] -- [AUDIO] -- 
[COMPOSITION] -- [STYLE]
```

### Example Prompt Structure

```
[ECU close-up] -- Slow push in. 85mm. -- Marcus Tyler -- Black man, late 20s, 
short beard, fade haircut, black jacket -- eyes reflecting amber code, intense 
focus, no blinking -- Cyberpunk command desk, monitors in background -- Warm 
amber key-light from monitors, cool blue fill from window -- Amber and blue 
contrast -- Mechanical keystrokes, low synth drone -- Centered framing, 
shallow DOF -- Cyberpunk thriller aesthetic, 35mm film grain, Blade Runner 
visual language.
```

### Reference Image Strategy

For consistent character appearance across shots:
1. Register character reference images (headshot, full body, profile)
2. Use `--no-auto-refs` to control exactly which references appear per shot
3. Include character references only when that character is on screen
4. Include location references for establishing shots
5. Include prop references when props are central to the action

### Aspect Ratio for Cinematic Feel

- **2.39:1** — True cinematic widescreen (1680x720). Use for: establishing shots, battles, scale.
- **16:9** — Standard widescreen. Use for: dialogue, medium shots, most scenes.
- **4:3** — Intimate, claustrophobic. Use for: close-ups, isolation, surveillance.
- **21:9** — Ultra-wide. Use for: panoramic, extreme scale, landscape.

**Recommendation:** Shoot in 16:9 and crop to 2.39:1 in post for maximum flexibility. The 2.39:1 frame forces compositional discipline.

## The Director's Checklist

Before approving any shot for production:

- [ ] Camera angle serves the emotion (not just "looks cool")
- [ ] Lens choice matches the emotional distance
- [ ] Movement has a reason (static = control, handheld = chaos)
- [ ] Composition follows widescreen rules (thirds, negative space)
- [ ] Lighting is motivated (practical sources exist in scene)
- [ ] Color language is consistent with character/act
- [ ] Audio strategy is defined (score, SFX, silence)
- [ ] Reference images are included for character consistency
- [ ] Aspect ratio is appropriate for the shot's function
- [ ] The shot advances the story or reveals character

## Continuity Object

Every film needs one object that exists across all worlds, all acts, all visual styles. It is the audience's anchor — the one thing that is always literally there.

**Rules:**
1. The object appears in EVERY act. Never disappear. Never be replaced.
2. The object transforms visually across styles (ink → line art → physical)
3. The object has its own arc — how the character relates to it changes
4. The object is introduced EARLY. By Scene 2 at the latest.
5. The object's final appearance mirrors its first — but changed.

**Examples:**
- A brush (ink tool → weapon → offering)
- A photograph (prized → ignored → understood)
- A key (locked → used → surrendered)
- A letter (unread → read → burned)

## Breath as Transition

When a brief says "she never crosses a portal, she crosses a breath," breath is the EDITING MECHANISM.

**How it works:**
- Act I ends: character EXHALES — next world appears ON THE EXHALE
- Act II ends: character INHALES — current world collapses ON THE INHALE
- Act III: character breathes NORMALLY — the real world IS the breath

**The cut is mapped to the breath.** Write: "She exhales. [CUT ON EXHALE.]" The editor knows exactly where to cut.

**Why it works:** The breath is universal. Every audience member breathes. When they see a breath on screen, they unconsciously match it. When the world changes on the breath, the audience FEELS the transition in their body, not just sees it with their eyes.

## Gradual Escalation

Never escalate all-at-once. The audience must feel walls closing ONE BY ONE.

**The Escalation Ladder:**
```
Step 1: Edges — far away. Character can still move freely.
Step 2: Near — closer now. Character adjusts. Still has space.
Step 3: Personal — touches character. Less room.
Step 4: Compression — surrounds character. Can't move freely.
Step 5: Breaking point — must choose. Fight, flee, or surrender.
```

Each step is a SEPARATE BEAT. Don't combine. Don't skip to the end.

## Reveal Rules

A reveal that is EXPLAINED is exposition. A reveal that is SHOWN is cinema.

**Bad:** "The void was never metaphorical. It was a real studio."
**Good:** Camera pulls back. We see the CURVE of the cyclorama wall. The FLOOR. A LIGHT STAND. A sandbag. A C-clip. Real things in a real room.

**The reveal checklist:**
- [ ] SHOWN through visual/sensory detail, never explained
- [ ] Audience discovers it BEFORE the character
- [ ] At least one mundane, real-world detail confirms it
- [ ] The reveal is SILENT. No voiceover. No dialogue. Just the image.
- [ ] The reveal recontextualizes EVERYTHING that came before

## Mirror Scenes — One Small Change

When a later scene mirrors an earlier scene, ONE detail must change.

**The detail must be:**
- Physical (a hand position, an object state, a light direction)
- Small (subconscious, not consciously noticed)
- Visible (the audience can SEE it, even if they don't register it)

**Examples:**
- Scene 1: brush in right hand. Scene 15: brush in left hand.
- Scene 1: bristles ink-stained. Scene 15: bristles clean.
- Scene 1: eyes down. Scene 15: eyes up.
- Scene 1: single light source. Scene 15: multiple light sources.

## INT/EXT Designation

Every scene must have an INT/EXT designation:

| Designation | Meaning | Emotional Effect |
|-------------|---------|------------------|
| INT | Interior — walls, ceiling, contained | Claustrophobia, intimacy, control |
| EXT | Exterior — sky, open, exposed | Freedom, vulnerability, scale |
| INT/EXT | Threshold — doorway, window, between | Transition, decision, liminality |

**The designation is thematic.** A story about suppression might be entirely INT (contained). A story about freedom might move from INT to EXT. A story about acceptance might end at the threshold (INT/EXT).

## Sensory Architecture

A screenplay that describes events is a summary. A screenplay that describes SENSATION is cinema.

**The Tactile Rule:** Every scene must contain at least one detail the audience can FEEL:
- The weight of an object in a hand
- The temperature of air on skin
- The texture of a surface under fingers
- The sound of silence in a specific room
- The smell of dust in light (implied through visual)

**The Specificity Rule:** Never write "she holds the brush." Write:
- "She turns the brush in her fingers. The wood is smooth where her thumb rests. The ferrule is cold."
- "The brush is heavier than it looks. Goat hair bristles. Ink-stained. Real."

**The Stillness Rule:** When a character is still, something MUST move. A finger. An eyelash. The brush. A dust mote in light. The stillness must feel HELD, not empty.

## Scene-Level Audio Strategy

Every scene must define its audio:

| Audio Type | When to Use | Effect |
|------------|-------------|--------|
| Silence | After intensity, during revelation | The audience fills the void with their own emotion |
| Ambient only | Establishing, processing | Grounded, real, present |
| Score | Escalation, emotion | Guided feeling, controlled atmosphere |
| Sound design | Specific objects, environments | Tactile, immersive, sensory |
| Contrast | Loud → quiet → loud | Shock, release, rhythm |

**Silence after noise is different from silence as default.** Silence after noise creates a void the audience fills. Silence as default creates boredom.

**The rhythm of silence:** Map silence to the breath. Silence on inhale. Sound on exhale. The audience's breathing becomes the soundtrack.
